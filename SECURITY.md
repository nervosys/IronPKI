# Security policy

## Reporting a vulnerability

Report privately, not as a public issue. Open a GitHub security advisory on
`nervosys/IronPKI`, or contact the maintainers directly if you cannot.

Useful reports include the version or commit, what you observed, and the
smallest input that shows it. A failing test is the most useful form. A
certificate, CRL or OCSP response that is accepted and should not be, or a
bound that can be exceeded, is worth reporting on its own; you do not need a
working exploit.

A flaw in a cryptographic primitive, a key encoding or a signer belongs to
IronCrypto: report it to `nervosys/IronCrypto`. IronPKI implements none.

## What this project can and cannot claim

**IronPKI is not FIPS 140-3 validated**, and neither is IronCrypto, which it
builds on. No CMVP certificate exists for either.

**It has not been independently reviewed.** The evidence is the author's:

- requirements-based tests, traced in
  [docs/TRACEABILITY.md](docs/TRACEABILITY.md) and enforced by a test;
- mutation checks recorded in the commit messages: a rule was broken and a
  test was seen to fail;
- 103 cases generated by an independent implementation (PyCA
  `cryptography`): endorsement-key certificates, TLS server identities,
  paths and certificate signatures, with the five places this crate's
  verdict differs from the recorded one named and explained in the tests;
- two fuzz targets with AddressSanitizer (`fuzz/`), one of which fails if the
  two path validators disagree. The runs recorded there are short: five
  minutes each.

- tests against OpenSSL 3.5 in both directions
  (`tests/openssl_interop.rs`): each verifies the chains the other issues,
  honours the other's CRLs and reads the other's OCSP responses; and 63
  certificates under name constraints of every form evaluated here, and
  46 paths with certificate policies, mappings and policy constraints, 20
  of them with a caller's own policy inputs, get
  the same verdict from OpenSSL and from both validators. They cover what
  OpenSSL will issue from its command line, which is well-formed.
- NIST's PKITS, sections 4.8 to 4.12: 80 certificate policy cases with the
  suite's own inputs and expected results, all judged as the suite says;
  six more end in a CA certificate, which is refused before its policies
  are looked at (`tests/vectors/pkits`);
- certificate policy processing compared with RFC 5280's own procedure,
  the policy tree written out as a test, on 300,000 paths drawn from a
  fixed sequence, half of them with a caller's inputs drawn too.

Most of the code and its tests came from IronSocketLayer 0.3.4, where it was
also exercised against public servers through TLS handshakes. **That has not
been repeated here.**

One part has had a second reading, which is not a review by someone else:
`src/x509_policy.rs` was read against RFC 5280's text by another instance of
the model that wrote it, given the code and the RFC only. It found no wrong
verdict, in 18 million comparisons against a reference of its own, and two
defects in the work bound, which 0.2.1 fixes.

None of that substitutes for review by someone else.

## What it does not do

So that nothing is assumed:

- It fetches nothing: no issuer certificates, CRLs or OCSP responses.
- Certificate policies are processed with the caller's inputs, which
  default to RFC 5280's: any policy acceptable, none required. A caller
  may name up to 64 acceptable policies, and a report says which of them
  are valid, not what else was. Naming policies requires none by itself.
  Policy qualifiers are not examined. A pinned leaf has no path, so
  nothing is asked of its policies. A path whose policies take more than a
  fixed amount of work to evaluate is refused: a million list entries
  examined. Nothing is remembered between the questions asked of a path,
  so one whose policy mappings fan in at level after level can exhaust
  that although RFC 5280 would accept it.
- A certificate counts as self-issued, for the policy counts that exempt
  one, when its issuer and subject are the same bytes, not by the name
  comparison of RFC 5280 section 7.1. A name that matches only after
  normalisation is treated as not self-issued, which errs towards
  refusal.
- Name constraints are evaluated for dNSName, iPAddress, rfc822Name,
  directoryName and uniformResourceIdentifier. Any other form in a
  constraint fails the path closed.
- Distinguished names are not compared as RFC 5280 §7.1 has it in full:
  there is no Unicode normalisation or case folding. Text outside ASCII is
  the same only when its encoding is. Under a directoryName constraint that
  errs towards refusal: an excluded subtree that cannot be shown not to
  hold a name refuses the path, and a permitted one that cannot be shown to
  hold it does not cover it.
- Keys other than ECDSA P-256, P-384 and P-521, Ed25519, RSA and ML-DSA are
  refused as unsupported, including SLH-DSA and HSS/LMS keys that IronCrypto
  can verify with.
- SHA-1 and MD5 signatures are refused.
- A TPM endorsement-key certificate is validated as a path. Binding it to a
  TPM's public area, and credential activation, are the caller's.
